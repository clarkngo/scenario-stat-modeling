Section 1

The paper in one minute

If you remember nothing else, be able to say these five sentences out loud without notes.

  1. After a calm stretch, people assume tomorrow will be calm too. Markets don't work that way: turbulent days cluster, and big losses come far more often than a bell curve says.
  2. Banks summarize this risk with Value-at-Risk: a loss they expect to exceed on only 1 day in 100 (or 5 in 100).
  3. Our original model, GARCH(1,1), updates tomorrow's risk from yesterday's surprise and yesterday's risk, and assumes a bell curve.
  4. Our revised model, GJR-GARCH with Student-t errors, fixes two wrong assumptions: it lets bad days raise risk more than good days, and it allows crash-sized moves.
  5. We built both from scratch in NumPy, fitted them on 1990–2014, and tested them on 2015–2026. The revised model fits unseen data better and was caught off guard far less often in the 2020 crash.

Draft 1's abstract is written as a plan and states no results. The numbers on this page come from our working prototype (results/garch_results.md), so they may change slightly before the final paper.

Section 2

Four ideas to own

Each one is a plain observation about markets, then the technical name for it, then the evidence from our own data.

Volatility clustering

Rough days come in bunches

A wild day is usually followed by more wild days, and calm by calm. You can't predict whether tomorrow is up or down, but you can predict whether it will be big.

Our data: today's return barely predicts tomorrow's (correlation −0.03 in training), but today's squared return does (0.21 in training, 0.44 in the test period).

Heavy tails

Crashes are not once-in-a-universe events

A bell curve says a 4-standard-deviation day should happen about once every 60 years. The US market does it about one and a half times a year.

Our data: 0.60% of days moved beyond 4 standard deviations, about 95 times the 0.006% a normal curve allows. Kurtosis is 11.3 in training and 16.7 in testing (a bell curve has 3).

Leverage effect / asymmetry

Fear is louder than greed

A 3% drop makes markets much more nervous than a 3% rise. Same size of move, different reaction.

Our fit: the revised model's reaction to good surprises (α) is 0.001, essentially zero; its extra reaction to bad surprises (γ) is 0.141. After a −3% day it predicts 1.51% volatility; after a +3% day, 1.00%.

Persistence / mean reversion

Storms fade, slowly

After a shock, risk stays high for a while and then drifts back toward a normal level. It doesn't snap back overnight.

Our fit: persistence ≈ 0.99, so half of any shock to risk has faded after about 64–67 trading days (roughly three months).

Feel the model

This uses the exact parameters we fitted on 1990–2014. Pick today's market move and today's risk level, and see what each model expects for tomorrow. Try a −3% day and then a +3% day.

The return minus its average (\(e_t\)). Negative = a bad day.
What the model expected today (\(\sigma_t\)). Long-run level ≈ 1.05%.
Original: tomorrow's volatility
Revised: tomorrow's volatility
Original: 1% VaR (loss limit)
Revised: 1% VaR (loss limit)

How the numbers are made: tomorrow's variance = ω + (α + γ·[today was bad]) × surprise² + β × today's variance. The loss limit is average return + (1% quantile) × tomorrow's volatility, where the quantile is −2.326 for the bell curve and −2.509 for the fitted Student-t (ν ≈ 8).

Section 4

Glossary

Each entry has four parts: what it means in plain words, where it shows up in our paper, the math (if any), and a one-line answer if someone asks about it.

Data & returns

Daily return also: simple return, percent return

How much the market's value changed from one day's close to the next, as a percentage. +1.2 means the market rose 1.2% that day.

In our paper
Our \(r_t\). We use the total US market return in percent, built as Mkt-RF + RF from the French data. 9,233 trading days from 1990-01-02 to 2026-08-31.
Math
\( r_t = 100 \times \dfrac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \)
If asked
"We model daily percentage returns. Working in percent keeps the numbers near 1 instead of 0.0001, which helps the optimizer."

Simple vs. log return

Two ways to measure a day's change. A simple return is the percent change; a log return is the natural log of the price ratio. For daily moves of a percent or two they are almost identical.

In our paper
We use simple returns because that is what the French library publishes. Many GARCH papers use log returns; the choice barely matters at daily frequency.
Math
\( \ln(1 + x) \approx x \) when \(x\) is small, e.g. \( \ln(1.01) = 0.00995 \)
If asked
"At daily frequency the difference is in the third decimal place, so it doesn't change any conclusion."

Market excess return (Mkt-RF)

The market's return minus the return on a risk-free Treasury bill. It's what investors earn for taking stock-market risk.

In our paper
The French library publishes Mkt-RF. We add RF back to get the total market return, because Value-at-Risk is about the actual loss on the portfolio.
If asked
"Daily RF is about 0.01%, so adding it back has almost no effect on volatility; it just keeps the return honest."

Risk-free rate (RF)

The return on a very safe short-term US government bill. The French library uses the one-month Treasury bill.

In our paper
Added to Mkt-RF to form the total return. Tiny on a daily basis.

Kenneth R. French Data Library

A free, widely used research data set run by Professor Kenneth French at Dartmouth's Tuck School of Business. Its market return covers all US stocks listed on the NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ (from CRSP), weighted by company size.

In our paper
Our only data source. tools/fetch_french.py downloads and cleans it into data/french_daily.csv.
If asked
"It's public, documented and used in thousands of finance papers, so anyone can rerun our results. That's why we chose it over scraping Yahoo Finance."

Value-weighted

Each company counts in proportion to its total market value. Apple moves the index far more than a small company does.

In our paper
The French market return is value-weighted, so it behaves much like the S&P 500 but is slightly broader.

Training period / test period

We learn the model's settings from one stretch of history (training), then judge it on a later stretch it has never seen (test). Like studying past exams, then sitting a new one.

In our paper
Train: 1990-01-02 to 2014-12-31 (6,301 days; includes the 1998, 2000–02 and 2008 crises). Test: 2015-01-02 to 2026-08-31 (2,932 days; includes 2018, the 2020 COVID crash, 2022 and the April 2025 tariff swings).
If asked
"The split is by time, never random, because shuffling days would let the model peek at the future."

Out-of-sample

Results measured on data the model did not use for fitting. This is the honest test of a forecasting model.

In our paper
All backtests and log scores are on the 2015–2026 test period only.

Look-ahead bias

Accidentally using information that wasn't available at the time. It makes a model look better than it could have been in real life.

In our paper
Avoided: parameters (ω, α, γ, β, ν, μ) come from training only, and each day's forecast uses only returns up to the day before.
If asked
"Every forecast for day t uses returns through day t−1 and parameters frozen at the end of 2014."

Stress window

A short period of extreme turbulence we zoom in on, to see how each model behaves when it matters most.

In our paper
February–April 2020 (COVID crash), including the −12.0% day on 2020-03-16.

Trading day

A day the stock exchange is open. There are about 252 per year, not 365.

In our paper
All "days" (half-life, rolling window, test length) are trading days. 250 days ≈ one trading year.

Risk & volatility

Volatility also: σ (sigma), standard deviation

How big the market's typical daily move is, up or down. It measures size, not direction.

In our paper
The quantity we forecast, \(\sigma_t\). The market's average daily volatility is about 1.1% (≈ 18% a year).
Math
\( \sigma = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^2} \)
If asked
"We don't forecast whether the market goes up or down. We forecast how big tomorrow's move is likely to be."

Variance σ²

Volatility squared. Models work in variance because it is always positive and adds up neatly; we take the square root at the end to talk in percent.

In our paper
GARCH equations are written for \(\sigma_t^2\). Forecasts are reported as \(\sigma_t\).

Annualized volatility

Daily volatility scaled up to a yearly figure, which is how it's usually quoted.

In our paper
1.135% daily × √252 ≈ 18.0% a year for training; 18.1% for testing.
Math
\( \sigma_{\text{year}} = \sigma_{\text{day}} \sqrt{252} \)
If asked
"The √252 rule assumes days are independent, which GARCH says isn't quite true, so it's a convention, not a law."

Conditional vs. unconditional variance

Unconditional: the long-run average risk, ignoring today's news. Conditional: the risk for tomorrow given everything we know today. GARCH is a model of conditional variance.

In our paper
\(\sigma_t^2\) is conditional. The long-run variance \(\omega/(1-\text{persistence})\) is unconditional.
If asked
"A weather forecast is conditional (it uses today's sky); the climate average is unconditional."

Heteroskedasticity also: heteroscedasticity

A long word for "the spread changes over time". Conditional heteroskedasticity means the spread changes in a way we can predict from the past. The "CH" in ARCH and GARCH stands for this.

In our paper
The core property of stock returns that makes a constant-volatility model wrong.
If asked
"Hetero = different, skedastic = spread. Risk isn't constant, and yesterday tells you something about tomorrow."

Volatility clustering

Big moves tend to follow big moves, and small follow small. The signature pattern GARCH was built to capture.

In our paper
Lag-1 autocorrelation of returns is −0.03 (train), but of squared returns is 0.21 (train) and 0.44 (test).
If asked
"Direction is close to unpredictable; size is predictable. That's the whole opening for GARCH."

Autocorrelation

How much a series is correlated with its own past values. Near 0 means yesterday tells you little about today.

In our paper
Used as evidence for clustering: near zero for returns, clearly positive for squared and absolute returns.
Math
\( \rho_1 = \text{corr}(x_t, x_{t-1}) \)

Mean reversion (of volatility)

After a spike, risk drifts back toward its normal long-run level rather than staying high forever.

In our paper
Guaranteed when persistence < 1. Speed is summarized by the half-life.

Stylized facts

Patterns that show up in almost every financial market and period: heavy tails, volatility clustering, asymmetry, near-zero autocorrelation of returns. Cont (2001) is the standard summary.

In our paper
Each revision targets a stylized fact the original model ignores: asymmetry (→ γ) and heavy tails (→ Student-t).
If asked
"We didn't invent the revisions; we picked them because they address well-documented facts about returns."

Leverage effect also: asymmetric volatility

Bad news raises future volatility more than good news of the same size. Named because falling prices make companies more indebted relative to their value, though fear and selling pressure are also part of the story.

In our paper
Captured by γ in GJR-GARCH. Our fitted γ = 0.141 vs α = 0.001.
If asked
"In our data, good surprises add almost nothing to tomorrow's risk. Only bad surprises do."

Kurtosis / excess kurtosis

A measure of how heavy the tails are, meaning how often extreme moves happen. A bell curve has kurtosis 3 ("excess kurtosis" 0).

In our paper
11.3 in training and 16.7 in testing, so the test period was even more extreme than training.
Math
\( \kappa = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t z_t^4, \quad z_t = (r_t - \bar r)/\sigma \)
If asked
"Kurtosis of 16 means extreme days are far more common than a normal curve predicts. That's why we switched to the Student-t."

Skewness

Whether the distribution leans to one side. Negative skew means large drops are more common than equally large rises.

In our paper
−0.12 (train), −0.40 (test). Our Student-t is symmetric, so it does not capture skew; a skewed-t is a possible next revision.

Distributions

Normal distribution also: Gaussian, bell curve

The familiar symmetric bell shape. Most values sit near the middle and extremes are very rare.

In our paper
The original model assumes each day's standardized shock is normal. That is the assumption the revised model drops.
Math
\( f(z) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2} \)

Heavy tails also: fat tails

Extreme values happen much more often than a bell curve predicts.

In our paper
0.60% of days beyond 4σ vs 0.006% under a normal curve. The fitted Student-t (ν ≈ 8) allows 0.17%, which is much closer but still short.
If asked
"Even the Student-t underestimates the most extreme days. It's an improvement, not a cure."

Student-t distribution

A bell-shaped curve with heavier tails, controlled by one setting, ν. Small ν means heavy tails; as ν grows it becomes the normal curve.

In our paper
The revised model's shock distribution, following Bollerslev (1987). Fitted ν ≈ 8.0.
If asked
"It's the smallest change that gives crash days realistic weight: one extra parameter, and it nests the normal as a special case."

Degrees of freedom ν (nu)

The Student-t's tail-heaviness knob. ν = 3–5 is very heavy, ν ≈ 8 is moderately heavy, ν = ∞ is the normal curve.

In our paper
ν ≈ 8.0 for GJR-t, 7.1 for GARCH-t. We force ν > 2 so the variance exists.
Math
\( \nu = 2 + e^{z} \) (so it can never go below 2)
If asked
"The data chose ν ≈ 8 by maximum likelihood; we didn't set it by hand."

Standardized Student-t

A Student-t rescaled so its variance is exactly 1. That keeps σt meaning "volatility" in both models, so the comparison is fair.

In our paper
Used for \(z_t\) in the t models.
Math
\( z = t_\nu \sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu} \)

PDF and CDF

The PDF (density) gives how likely each value is. The CDF gives the probability of being at or below a value.

In our paper
The PDF goes into the likelihood. The CDF is inverted to get VaR quantiles.
Math
\( F(x) = P(Z \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(u)\,du \)

Quantile also: percentile, inverse CDF

The value that a given share of outcomes fall below. The 1% quantile is the cutoff where only 1 day in 100 is worse.

In our paper
1% quantile: −2.326 (normal) vs −2.509 (standardized t, ν = 8). 5% quantile: −1.645 vs −1.610.
If asked
"Note the crossover: the t is more extreme at 1% but slightly less extreme at 5%. Heavy tails move probability to both the far tails and the center."

i.i.d.

"Independent and identically distributed": each draw comes from the same distribution and doesn't depend on the others.

In our paper
We assume the standardized shocks \(z_t\) are i.i.d. The returns themselves are not, because their volatility changes.

The models

Shock also: innovation, surprise, et

How far today's return was from its average. The "news" the model reacts to.

In our paper
\(\mu\) is the training-sample average daily return (0.0435%).
Math
\( e_t = r_t - \mu = \sigma_t z_t \)

Standardized residual zt

The shock divided by the predicted volatility. It answers "how surprising was today, given how risky we thought it would be?"

Math
\( z_t = e_t / \sigma_t \)
If asked
"A −3% day is a 3-sigma shock in a calm market but only a 1-sigma shock in a crisis."

ARCH

AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (Engle, 1982). Tomorrow's variance depends on recent squared shocks. Engle later shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Economics for it.

Math
\( \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1 e_{t-1}^2 + \dots + \alpha_q e_{t-q}^2 \)
If asked
"ARCH needs many lags to capture slow-fading risk. GARCH does it with one extra term."

GARCH(1,1) our original model

Generalized ARCH (Bollerslev, 1986). Tomorrow's risk = a small baseline + a reaction to today's surprise + most of today's risk carried forward. "(1,1)" means one lag of the surprise and one lag of the variance.

In our paper
With normal shocks. Fitted ω = 0.0123, α = 0.084, β = 0.906.
Math
\( \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha e_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2 \)
If asked
"It's a recursive, nonlinear model of a quantity we never observe directly, fitted by maximum likelihood. That's why it isn't a regression."

ω (omega)

The baseline: a small constant amount of variance added every day. It sets the level risk drifts back to.

In our paper
0.0123 (original), 0.0116 (revised). Must be > 0.

α (alpha)

The reaction: how much today's surprise (squared) raises tomorrow's variance. In GJR it applies to all surprises, good or bad.

In our paper
0.084 (original). 0.001 (revised), because the revised model moves the reaction to γ.
If asked
"α ≈ 0 in the revised model doesn't mean the model ignores news. It means good news adds nothing beyond what bad news adds through γ."

β (beta)

The memory: how much of today's variance carries over to tomorrow. Close to 1 means risk fades slowly.

In our paper
0.906 (original), 0.918 (revised).
If asked
"This β is not the CAPM market beta. Here it's the memory of volatility."

GJR-GARCH(1,1) our revised model (with Student-t)

GARCH with one extra term (Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle, 1993) that adds more risk only after a bad day.

In our paper
Fitted ω = 0.0116, α = 0.001, γ = 0.141, β = 0.918, ν = 8.0.
Math
\( \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \big(\alpha + \gamma\, \mathbb{1}[e_{t-1}<0]\big) e_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2 \)
If asked
"Set γ = 0 and you get the original GARCH back. The revision is a strict extension, so the comparison is clean."

γ (gamma)

The extra reaction to bad surprises only. If γ > 0, a drop raises risk more than a rise of the same size.

In our paper
0.141. After a bad day the total reaction is α + γ ≈ 0.142; after a good day it's α ≈ 0.001.

Indicator function 𝟙[·]

A switch that equals 1 when the condition is true and 0 otherwise.

In our paper
\(\mathbb{1}[e_{t-1}<0]\) turns γ on only after a bad day.

Persistence

How much of a shock to risk survives from one day to the next. Close to 1 means storms fade slowly.

In our paper
0.9898 (original), 0.9892 (revised). For GJR, γ counts half because bad days happen about half the time.
Math
GARCH: \( \alpha + \beta \). GJR: \( \alpha + \gamma/2 + \beta \)
If asked
"Persistence of 0.99 is typical for daily stock returns in the literature, which is one sign our fit is sensible."

Half-life

How many days until half of a shock to risk has faded away.

In our paper
67 days (original), 64 days (revised): about three months.
Math
\( h = \dfrac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(\text{persistence})} \)

Long-run variance also: unconditional variance

The level of risk the model drifts back to when nothing unusual happens.

In our paper
Long-run daily volatility 1.10% (original), 1.04% (revised); about 16–17% a year.
Math
\( \bar\sigma^2 = \dfrac{\omega}{1 - \text{persistence}} \)

Stationarity (covariance stationarity)

The model's risk doesn't explode over time; it has a finite long-run level. Requires persistence below 1.

In our paper
Enforced by construction through the reparameterization, so every fitted model is stationary.
Math
\( \omega > 0,\ \alpha,\gamma,\beta \ge 0,\ \alpha + \gamma/2 + \beta < 1 \)

News impact curve

A picture of tomorrow's volatility as a function of today's surprise, starting from a normal day. GARCH draws a symmetric U; GJR draws a lopsided one.

In our paper
Figure fig_garch_news.png. Revised model: −3% day → 1.51%; +3% day → 1.00%. Original: 1.36% either way.
If asked
"This one picture is the asymmetry finding. The right half of the orange curve is almost flat."

EWMA / RiskMetrics

Exponentially weighted moving average: recent days count more, older days fade at a fixed rate. Introduced as an industry standard by J.P. Morgan's RiskMetrics (1996) with λ = 0.94.

In our paper
A benchmark. It's GARCH with ω = 0, α = 0.06, β = 0.94, so persistence = 1 and there is no long-run level to return to.
Math
\( \sigma_t^2 = (1-\lambda) e_{t-1}^2 + \lambda \sigma_{t-1}^2 \)
If asked
"EWMA has nothing fitted. GARCH learns its settings from data and adds mean reversion."

Rolling-window volatility our naive benchmark

Just the standard deviation of the last 250 trading days (about one year). What most people would do without a model.

In our paper
The "calm year" trap: it reacts slowly going into a crisis and stays too cautious after it.

Nested models

One model is a special case of another. GARCH-normal is GJR-t with γ = 0 and ν = ∞.

In our paper
We fit all four combinations (GARCH/GJR × normal/t) so we can see which revision helps and by how much.
If asked
"Fitting the in-between models shows each fix pulls its weight on its own."

Fitting the models

Likelihood

For a given set of parameter values: how probable the data we actually saw would be. Better parameters make the real history look less surprising.

Math
\( L(\theta) = \prod_t f(r_t \mid r_1,\dots,r_{t-1};\theta) \)
If asked
"We pick the settings under which 25 years of real market history is least surprising."

Log-likelihood / negative log-likelihood

The log turns a product of tiny numbers into a sum, which computers handle better. We minimize the negative log-likelihood because optimizers look for minimums.

In our paper
Training −log L: 8,395.6 (original) vs 8,199.5 (revised). Lower is better.
Math (normal)
\( -\ell = \tfrac12 \sum_t \Big[\ln 2\pi + \ln \sigma_t^2 + \dfrac{e_t^2}{\sigma_t^2}\Big] \)

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

Choosing parameters that make the likelihood as large as possible.

In our paper
How all four GARCH variants are fitted, with our own optimizer.
If asked
"We can't use least squares because volatility is never observed. We only see returns, so we fit the distribution of returns instead."

Student-t log-likelihood

The same idea as the normal version, but using the standardized t density. Big shocks are penalized less, so one crash day doesn't wreck the fit.

Math
\( \ell_t = \ln\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\big) - \ln\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{\nu}{2}\big) - \tfrac12\ln\!\big(\pi(\nu-2)\big) - \tfrac12 \ln\sigma_t^2 - \tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\ln\!\Big(1+\dfrac{e_t^2}{\sigma_t^2(\nu-2)}\Big) \)

Optimizer

An algorithm that searches for the parameter values giving the lowest (or highest) score.

In our paper
Our own Nelder–Mead in model/optim.py. SciPy is not allowed in the course.

Nelder–Mead simplex method

A search that needs no derivatives. It keeps a small cluster of trial points (a "simplex"), throws away the worst one and replaces it by reflecting, stretching, pulling in or shrinking the cluster, until all points agree.

In our paper
Nelder & Mead (1965). Stops when the best and worst trial points' scores differ by less than 1e-10 (relative), or after 5,000 steps.
If asked
"With only 4–6 parameters it's reliable and needs no gradient, which matters because the GARCH recursion makes gradients messy to derive and code by hand."

Simplex moves: reflect, expand, contract, shrink

Reflect: flip the worst point through the middle of the others. Expand: if that worked very well, go twice as far. Contract: if it failed, try halfway back. Shrink: if all else fails, pull everything toward the best point.

In our paper
Coefficients 1 (reflect), 2 (expand), 0.5 (contract), 0.5 (shrink), the textbook values.

Restarts

Running the search again from the best point found, with a smaller starting cluster, to make sure it didn't stall early.

In our paper
Up to 3 runs, halving the step each time, stopping when the score stops improving.

Reparameterization

Letting the optimizer search over unrestricted numbers, then converting them into parameters that automatically obey the rules (positive, persistence < 1, ν > 2).

In our paper
Tested on 1,000 random draws: the constraints always hold.
Math
\( \omega = e^{z_0},\quad [\alpha,\ \tfrac{\gamma}{2},\ \beta] = \dfrac{e^{z_{1:3}}}{1 + \sum e^{z_{1:3}}},\quad \nu = 2 + e^{z_4} \)
If asked
"Instead of rejecting bad guesses, we made bad guesses impossible."

Starting values and initial variance

Where the search begins, and what variance the recursion uses on day one.

In our paper
Start near typical values (α ≈ 0.08, persistence ≈ 0.98, ν ≈ 6). Day-one variance = training sample variance (1.288).

Local vs. global optimum

A local optimum is the best point nearby; the global optimum is the best point anywhere. Searches can get stuck on a local one.

If asked
"We guard against this with sensible starting values, restarts, and a test showing the fit recovers known parameters from simulated data."

Parameter recovery test

Simulate data from a model with known settings, fit it, and check you get those settings back. If you can't recover the truth on fake data, you can't trust the fit on real data.

In our paper
8,000 simulated days with ω = 0.02, α = 0.03, γ = 0.12, β = 0.88, ν = 6. Our fit recovers γ within 0.04, β within 0.03, α within 0.03 and ν in 4.5–8.5 (model/test_garch.py).

AIC and BIC

Scores that reward fit but charge a penalty for each extra parameter, so a bigger model has to earn its complexity. Lower is better. BIC's penalty is stricter.

In our paper
AIC 16,799 → 16,411; BIC 16,826 → 16,451 (original → revised). k = 4 vs 6 parameters.
Math
\( \text{AIC} = 2(-\ell) + 2k, \quad \text{BIC} = 2(-\ell) + k\ln n \)
If asked
"The revised model has two more parameters, but its fit improves by far more than the penalty."

Likelihood-ratio comparison

Twice the gap in log-likelihood between a smaller and a bigger nested model. Large values mean the extra parameters really help.

In our paper
2 × (8,395.6 − 8,199.5) ≈ 392 for 2 extra parameters, far above the usual 5.99 cutoff.
If asked
"Strictly, ν = ∞ sits on the edge of the parameter space so the textbook χ² cutoff is only approximate, but 392 is far past any reasonable threshold."

Bisection

Find where a function crosses a target by repeatedly cutting the search interval in half.

In our paper
How we compute the normal quantile from the CDF (via math.erf) without SciPy. Accurate to 1e-8.

Numerical integration (trapezoid rule)

Approximate an area under a curve by slicing it into thin trapezoids and adding them up.

In our paper
How we get the Student-t CDF, and from it the quantile. We substitute \(x = \tan\theta\) so the infinite tail becomes a finite interval.
If asked
"It matches the published t-table value for ν = 5 to three decimals, and the normal quantile when ν is huge."

Vectorization vs. recursion

Vectorized code processes whole arrays at once (fast in NumPy). A recursion must go day by day because each step needs the previous one.

In our paper
The GARCH variance path is an unavoidable loop; the likelihood, benchmarks and backtests are vectorized.

Testing the forecasts

Value-at-Risk (VaR)

A loss threshold you expect to be crossed on only a small share of days. "1% daily VaR of −2.5%" means: on 99 days out of 100 we don't expect to lose more than 2.5%.

In our paper
Computed every test day for 1% and 5% levels as a return threshold.
Math
\( \text{VaR}_t^{(p)} = \mu + q_p\, \sigma_t \)
If asked

If asked
"We say 1% VaR. Banking regulation usually says 99% VaR. Same number."

Checking a risk model against what actually happened afterwards.

In our paper
Two questions: were there the right number of breaches (Kupiec), and did they arrive independently rather than in bunches (Christoffersen)?

In our paper
Kupiec (1995). At 5%, the revised model passes (p = 0.53). At 1%, every model fails (p < 0.001).
Math

"It only counts breaches. A model could pass while all its breaches arrive in one terrible week, so we also run Christoffersen."

p-value

If the model were exactly right, how often would we see a result at least this far off just by chance? Small (under 0.05) means "too unlikely to be luck", so we reject the model.

If asked
"A p-value is not the probability that the model is correct. A high p-value means the data doesn't contradict the model, not that we've proved it."

Likelihood-ratio test and χ² (chi-square)

Compare how well a "model is perfect" hypothesis explains the data against the best possible explanation. The chi-square distribution tells us how big that gap can get by chance.

In our paper

Section 6

Tough questions

Likely questions from the instructor or classmates, with answers you can give in your own words. Each is tagged with what it's really probing.

Is GARCH really "non-trivial"? Isn't it just a regression?course requirements

No. In a regression, the thing you predict is observed. Volatility is never observed; we only see returns. GARCH builds the volatility path through a nonlinear recursion where each day depends on the previous one, and it can only be fitted by maximum likelihood.

We derived the likelihoods, wrote the optimizer, the quantile functions and both backtests by hand in NumPy.

Why not a neural network or LSTM?model choice

Three reasons. The course requires building the math from scratch in NumPy, which is realistic for GARCH and not for a deep network. Every GARCH parameter has a plain meaning (reaction, memory, asymmetry, tails) that we can explain and check. And GARCH-family models are still the standard benchmark in risk management.

Hansen & Lunde (2005) compared 330 volatility models. GARCH(1,1) was hard to beat for exchange rates, but for stock returns it was beaten by models that allow a leverage effect. That is exactly our revision.

You forecast volatility, not prices. What is that useful for?motivation
  • Setting loss limits and capital (Value-at-Risk), which is what banks report to regulators.
  • Sizing positions: hold less when risk is high.
  • Pricing options, whose value depends directly on expected volatility.

Price direction is close to unpredictable; size of moves is predictable. We forecast the part that can be forecast.

Why did α drop to almost zero in the revised model?interpretation

Once the model is allowed a separate reaction to bad days (γ), the data puts nearly all the reaction there. α is the reaction to any surprise; γ is the extra for bad ones. α ≈ 0.001 and γ ≈ 0.141 means good surprises barely raise tomorrow's risk at all.

This matches Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle (1993), who found a similar pattern in US stock returns. It's our "fear is louder than greed" finding.

Every model fails the 1% test. Doesn't that mean your revision failed?honesty

The revision moved every metric in the right direction: 60 → 51 breaches, 6 → 2 breaches in the COVID crash, best log score of all six models. It narrowed the gap but didn't close it, and we say so.

Likely reasons the gap remains:

  • Parameters were frozen at the end of 2014 and never updated through 2026.
  • The test period was wilder than training (kurtosis 16.7 vs 11.3).
  • Even the Student-t with ν ≈ 8 under-predicts the most extreme days (0.17% beyond 4σ vs 0.60% observed).
  • Returns are negatively skewed; our t is symmetric.

Each points to a next revision: rolling re-estimation, a skewed-t, or adding Expected Shortfall.

Why do models pass at 5% but fail at 1%?interpretation

The 5% cutoff sits in the body of the distribution, where a bell curve is a decent approximation. The 1% cutoff sits in the tail, where real returns are much heavier than any of these models assume. Tails are where models struggle and also where the losses are.

At 5%, GJR-normal beats your revised model. Why not pick it?trap

At 5% both pass comfortably (p = 0.84 vs 0.53), so neither is rejected. The Student-t matters most deep in the tail: at 1% the revised model has fewer breaches (51 vs 58), far fewer in the crash (2 vs 5), and a better log score (1.287 vs 1.316). We chose the model that is best overall and in the tail, where risk management cares most.

A side fact worth knowing: the t's 5% quantile (−1.610) is slightly less extreme than the normal's (−1.645). Heavy tails put more probability in both the far tail and the center, so the t VaR is a bit tighter at 5% and looser at 1%.

The rolling rule had the most cautious average VaR but the most breaches. How?aha moment

Timing. The 250-day window reacts slowly: going into a crash it still reflects the calm year before, so it breaches repeatedly (12 times in Feb–Apr 2020). After the crash it remembers the turmoil for a full year and stays too cautious when markets have calmed. Being cautious on average doesn't help if the caution shows up at the wrong time.

That's the "a calm year makes you underestimate tomorrow" message, shown with real numbers.

Is there look-ahead bias?methodology

No. All parameters (μ, ω, α, γ, β, ν) are fitted on 1990–2014 only. Each test-day forecast uses only returns through the previous day. The test data never touches the fitting step.

Why not re-estimate the model every day or every year?methodology

We kept one fit to make the comparison simple and the test harder: if the revised model wins with settings frozen in 2014, it's not because it was re-tuned. Rolling re-estimation is a standard next step and would likely reduce breaches for all models.

Why 1990–2014 for training and 2015–2026 for testing?design

Training covers 25 years and several crises (1998, the 2000–02 dot-com crash, 2008), so the model has seen bad times. Testing covers over 11 years the model never saw, including 2018, the COVID crash, the 2022 sell-off and April 2025. About two-thirds train, one-third test.

Why Nelder–Mead instead of gradient descent or BFGS?implementation

It needs no derivatives. Gradients of the GARCH likelihood have to run back through the whole recursion, which is error-prone to derive and code by hand. With only 4–6 parameters, Nelder–Mead is reliable and fast enough. SciPy's optimizers aren't allowed in the course, so we wrote it ourselves (about 30 lines).

How do you know the optimizer found the real maximum?implementation
  • Restarts from the best point with a smaller simplex until the score stops improving.
  • A parameter-recovery test on 8,000 simulated days with known settings.
  • Results are in the typical range for daily US stocks (persistence ≈ 0.99, ν ≈ 7–8).
  • The likelihood ordering of the four variants is consistent across models.
How do you keep the parameters valid (positive, stationary)?implementation

Reparameterization. The optimizer searches over unrestricted numbers, which we convert using exponentials and a softmax-like share so that ω > 0, every coefficient ≥ 0, α + γ/2 + β < 1 and ν > 2 hold automatically. A test checks this on 1,000 random draws.

Why the Student-t and not another heavy-tailed distribution?model choice

It's the standard choice since Bollerslev (1987), adds just one parameter, and contains the normal as a limit (ν → ∞), so the comparison stays clean. A skewed-t would also capture the negative skew we measured (−0.40 in testing) and is a reasonable next revision.

Is the improvement statistically significant?don't overclaim

In-sample, clearly: the likelihood ratio is about 392 for two extra parameters, and AIC and BIC both drop by roughly 380–390. Out-of-sample, the revised model wins on breaches, crash breaches and log score, but we haven't yet run a formal test of the log-score gap (a Diebold–Mariano test). That's planned.

What does ν ≈ 8 actually mean?interpretation

Moderately heavy tails. Under a normal curve a 4-sigma day happens about 0.006% of the time; under our fitted t, about 0.17%, roughly 27 times more often. Real data shows 0.60%, so the t is much closer than the normal but still short.

What does a persistence of 0.99 mean in practice?interpretation

Each day, about 99% of the extra risk from a shock carries into the next day. Half of it is gone after about 64 trading days, roughly three months. That's why risk stayed elevated for months after March 2020.

Is β here the same as a stock's beta?trap

No. CAPM beta measures how a stock moves with the market. GARCH β is the memory of volatility: how much of today's variance carries over to tomorrow. Same Greek letter, unrelated ideas.

Why include EWMA and a rolling window?design

They show what practitioners do without a fitted model. The rolling window is the simplest "past year" approach. EWMA (RiskMetrics, λ = 0.94) is a longtime industry standard and turns out to be GARCH with ω = 0 and persistence fixed at 1. Beating them shows that fitting from data adds value.

Why the French data and not Yahoo Finance or the S&P 500?data

It's free, documented, stable and cited across finance research, so the results are reproducible. Yahoo's download tool is unofficial and changes without notice. FRED provides only the last 10 years of S&P 500 data. The French market return covers all US-listed stocks weighted by size, so it behaves much like the S&P 500.

Your results are for one market. Do they generalize?limitations

Fair point, and we say so. The patterns we exploit (clustering, heavy tails, asymmetry) are documented across many equity markets (Cont, 2001), so we expect the direction to hold. A planned robustness check reruns the backtest on the 12 French industry portfolios.

GARCH is from 1986. What's new in your paper?contribution
  • A complete, transparent from-scratch implementation: likelihoods, optimizer, quantiles and backtests in NumPy only.
  • A clean original-vs-revised comparison that isolates each fix (four nested models).
  • A test period through August 2026, including COVID and the 2025 swings.
  • A plain-language explanation of why each fix works, aimed at learners.
Why model returns in percent?implementation

Numerical stability. In decimals, daily variance is around 0.0001 and ω around 0.000001, which makes the optimizer's job harder. In percent, the numbers are near 1.

Why is the mean return μ held constant?assumptions

Daily expected returns are tiny (0.04%) compared with daily volatility (1.1%), and return autocorrelation is near zero. Modeling the mean in more detail would barely change the VaR. Volatility is what moves.

What does VaR not tell you?limitations

How bad the loss is once the limit is crossed. A 1% VaR of −2.5% is consistent with a −3% or a −12% day. Expected Shortfall (the average loss beyond VaR) addresses this, and is a natural extension.
