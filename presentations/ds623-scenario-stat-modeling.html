Forecasting Market Risk from Scratch

Revising GARCH into an asymmetric, heavy-tailed model for Value-at-Risk

Clark Jason Ngo & Manas Teja Katakam — MS in Data Science, City University of Seattle
Problem

A calm year makes tomorrow look calm

At the start of February 2020, the US market had just had a quiet year (13% annualized volatility vs. an 18% long-run average). A risk limit based on the past year allowed for a worst day of about −1.8%.

  • Next three months — 13 days with losses worse than −3%; −12.0% on March 16
  • Value-at-Risk (VaR) — a daily loss limit that should be broken on only 1 day in 100 (or 5 in 100)
  • Research question — can a volatility model built from first principles, then revised where real data proves it wrong, give better limits?
Background · Cont (2001)

Direction is unpredictable. Size is not.

Volatility clustering
Rough days come in bunches. Lag-1 autocorrelation: returns −0.03, squared returns 0.21 (train) and 0.44 (test).
Heavy tails
Kurtosis 11.3 (train), 16.7 (test); a bell curve has 3. Days beyond 4σ: 0.60% vs 0.006% under a normal.
Asymmetry
A drop makes markets more nervous than a rise of the same size: the leverage effect (Glosten et al., 1993).

Data: Kenneth R. French Data Library, daily US market return, 1990-01-02 to 2026-08-31 (9,233 trading days).

Original model · Bollerslev (1986)

GARCH(1,1): tomorrow's risk from today's surprise

\[ r_t = \mu + \sigma_t z_t,\quad z_t \sim N(0,1) \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\, e_{t-1}^2 + \beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2 \]
  • ω baseline, α reaction, β memory — β here is not CAPM beta
  • Persistence α + β — fitted 0.990: half of a shock fades in ≈ 67 trading days
  • Fitted values — ω = 0.0123, α = 0.084, β = 0.906; long-run volatility 1.10% a day
Estimation

Maximum likelihood, with our own optimizer

\[ -\ell = \tfrac12 \sum_t \Big[\ln 2\pi + \ln\sigma_t^2 + \frac{e_t^2}{\sigma_t^2}\Big] \]
\[ \omega = e^{z_0}, \qquad [\alpha,\ \tfrac{\gamma}{2},\ \beta] = \frac{e^{z_{1:3}}}{1 + \sum e^{z_{1:3}}}, \qquad \nu = 2 + e^{z_4} \]
  • Why likelihood — volatility is never observed, so least squares has nothing to fit
  • Nelder–Mead (1965) — derivative-free simplex search, ~30 lines of NumPy, with restarts
  • Reparameterization — every trial point is automatically valid; recovers known parameters on 8,000 simulated days
Test on a crisis

The original model is surprised too often

  • 60 breaches of the 1% limit in 2,932 test days, about twice the 29 expected
  • 6 breaches in Feb–Apr 2020 (past-year rule: 12)
  • The past-year rule is the most cautious on average, yet breaks the most: its caution arrives at the wrong time

Revised model · Glosten et al. (1993), Bollerslev (1987)

Relax exactly the two assumptions that failed

\[ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \big(\alpha + \gamma\, \mathbb{1}[e_{t-1} < 0]\big)\, e_{t-1}^2 + \beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2, \qquad z_t \sim t_\nu \sqrt{\tfrac{\nu-2}{\nu}} \]
  • γ: bad news counts more — extra reaction only after a negative surprise; persistence α + γ/2 + β
  • Student-t shocks — one extra parameter ν gives crash days realistic weight
  • Strict extension — γ = 0 and ν → ∞ gives the original back; we fit all four combinations
Finding

Fear is louder than greed

  • α = 0.001, γ = 0.141 — good surprises barely move tomorrow's risk
  • After a −3% day — revised model: 1.51% volatility; original: 1.36%
  • After a +3% day — revised model: 1.00%; original: still 1.36%
  • ν ≈ 8 — 1% quantile −2.51 instead of −2.33

Discussion

The revision narrows the gap; it doesn't close it

  • Frozen parameters — estimated once on data ending in 2014
  • A wilder test period — kurtosis 16.7 vs 11.3 in training
  • Tails still too thin — the fitted t allows 0.17% of 4σ days; 0.6% occurred
  • Skew — returns lean negative (−0.40); the t is symmetric
  • Next — rolling re-estimation, skewed-t, Expected Shortfall, a significance test of the log-score gain, 12 industry portfolios
Lessons learned

Revise the assumption that broke, then measure the fix

Look at the data before choosing a model. When the thing you care about is never observed, use likelihood. Judge forecasts on data the model hasn't seen, and report the failures too.
